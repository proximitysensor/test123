Doing Nothing is Also a Choice

You Think You're Making Choices Online. That's Adorable. Were you born yesterday? Every time you watch, read, or buy something, you might believe you're making independent choices, but sophisticated algorithms have invested billions of calculations to anticipate your preferences. And here's the thing: knowing this doesn't really change your behavior, does it?

Consider TikTok's "For You" page. On the surface, it suggests it's personalization for your benefit—after all, it is for you. But is it really? MIT Technology Review in 2021 noted it was "uncannily good at keeping users glued to their screens." Big Tech craves our attention, but does it deserve it? You can see the manipulation happening in real-time and still find yourself scrolling for hours. Awareness doesn't stop the scrolling—it just adds a layer of ironic self-commentary to your digital consumption.

Let's talk about your news diet. In 2015, Columbia University researchers found that 59% of social media links are shared without being clicked.. That's right: most people share headlines without reading the article—curious behavior, isn't it? You might pride yourself on being more discerning, recognizing when headlines are designed to trigger emotional reactions. Yet how often do you still click those provocative headlines, even while thinking, "I see what they're doing here"?

The platforms certainly know what they're doing. Have you noticed how certain types of headlines always seem to rise to the top of your feed? It's almost as if there's an invisible hand guiding what catches your attention. These systems weren't designed by accident—they're engineered to maximize your engagement rather than your understanding. You might recognize the pattern, roll your eyes, and then engage anyway. Because knowing the game doesn't mean you stop playing it.

While most users believe they're simply seeing what's important or popular, the reality might be more calculated. Those emotionally charged, polarizing posts that keep appearing? They generate clicks, shares, and valuable data about your reactions. Convenient how this serves the advertising model, isn't it? When you notice this pattern and still engage, you've achieved peak digital enlightenment: acknowledging the manipulation while voluntarily participating in it.

But surely you're different from the average user. You probably read every article before sharing, carefully considering its accuracy and context... don't you? Ever heard of the "filter bubble"? Eli Pariser coined the term to describe how algorithms reinforce your existing beliefs by feeding you more of what you already like. In fact, a 2019 research study showed YouTube’s recommendation engine could funnel users toward radical content—even if they weren't looking for it. But that's just the algorithm helping you become the most "you" you can be. Why settle for a broad worldview when you can have a worldview that fits like a custom-made echo chamber? "I know this is just reinforcing my existing beliefs," you think, as you click anyway.

And if you've ever tried to cancel a subscription and failed, congratulations! You've encountered "dark patterns"—those little UX flourishes designed to make you buy things you don't want and keep you from leaving. In 2019, Princeton researchers found over 10% of shopping sites used these tricks. But hey, if you didn't want that extra streaming service, you wouldn't have clicked "subscribe" seven times, right? Even when you recognize the dark pattern, you often just sigh and continue—because the alternative seems like too much effort.

So, what's the solution? Should you delete your apps, switch browsers, or rethink your relationship with technology? Absolutely not. That sounds exhausting.

Instead, here's the ultimate fix: The next time your feed recommends something, pause and think, "Wow, the algorithm thinks I'd like this." Then click it anyway. Because if you notice the manipulation, you've basically beaten it. Awareness is the new autonomy.

Problem solved.
