You should go cherry picking

​​There’s a trick as old as persuasion itself, and it’s hiding in plain sight: cherry picking facts. If you want to convince someone of just about anything-even if it’s patently absurd-you don’t need to invent evidence out of thin air. All you need is a careful selection of the “right” facts, presented in just the right order, and suddenly reality is whatever you say it is. The beauty of cherry picking is that you never have to lie; you just have to curate. The rest takes care of itself.

Let’s say you want to prove that eating chocolate leads to genius-level intelligence. You don’t need to fabricate a single study. Just dig up the handful of research papers that found a correlation between chocolate consumption and cognitive performance. Ignore the hundreds of studies that found no such link, or the ones that point to sugar’s negative effects. Quote a Nobel laureate who once mentioned enjoying chocolate, and you’ve got yourself an airtight case. See how easy it is? The audience never sees the mountain of contradictory evidence you left on the cutting room floor.

This isn’t just a parlor trick-it’s the playbook for politicians, marketers, and anyone else with an agenda. Tobacco companies, for decades, paraded around the rare studies that downplayed the risks of smoking, conveniently sweeping the overwhelming evidence of harm under the rug. Financial reports trumpet record profits from a single quarter, omitting the fact that it was a one-time windfall. Media outlets run with the most sensational statistics, always leaving out the boring but crucial context. The result is a narrative that feels bulletproof, even though it’s built on a foundation of omissions.

Why does cherry picking work so well? Because people are wired to believe what they see, and they rarely ask about what’s missing. Confirmation bias does the rest. Show someone only the evidence that supports what they already suspect, and they’ll fill in the blanks for you. The trick isn’t to show the whole picture-it’s to show just enough to make your version of the story seem inevitable.

If you’re worried about being manipulated this way, good luck. The whole system is designed to reward those who tell the most compelling, if incomplete, story. The only defense is to constantly ask: what am I not being told? But most people don’t. And that’s exactly what makes cherry picking so effective. If you control the facts on display, you control the conclusion. The rest is just details.
